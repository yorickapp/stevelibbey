Healthcare UX is not about making things look clean. It is about making the right action obvious when someone is under pressure, distracted, and responsible for a life.

Most of what passes for healthcare design is visual improvement work applied to systems whose fundamental structure is wrong. The interface looks better. The workflow is unchanged. The provider still has to navigate four screens to document a medication change and another three to notify the relevant care team members. The cognitive load is identical. The risk is identical.

## The Clinical Context

Clinicians operate in an environment that consumer UX designers rarely encounter: high stakes, genuine time pressure, incomplete information, and frequent interruption. A nurse documenting in an EHR may be interrupted twice in the time it takes to complete a single entry. A physician reviewing a patient's medication list during rounds may have thirty seconds before the team needs to move.

These are not edge cases to design around. They are the baseline condition. If the tool only works when the user has uninterrupted focus, it doesn't work.

## What Cognitive Load Actually Means

Cognitive load in clinical settings is not about complexity of the interface. It is about the total mental work the clinician is doing, of which the interface is only one part. They are simultaneously managing the patient's immediate situation, communicating with team members, tracking multiple patients, and trying to document accurately.

The interface should reduce that load, not add to it. That means fewer clicks for high-frequency tasks. It means putting critical information where the eye goes first without requiring the user to scroll or navigate. It means making the most common action the default, not the most comprehensive one.

## The 30-Second Rule

I have used a simple test when evaluating clinical interfaces: can a clinician accomplish the most common task in this workflow in 30 seconds or less, starting from the moment they open the application? If the answer is no, the interface has failed regardless of how it scores on usability heuristics.

This rule is not arbitrary. It reflects the actual time budget available in clinical environments. If a task takes longer than 30 seconds, clinicians find workarounds. They document from memory at the end of the shift. They skip steps. They use paper. The interface becomes an obstacle rather than a tool.

## Trust and Error Prevention

Healthcare interfaces carry a specific trust requirement that consumer products do not: the cost of error is sometimes a patient outcome. Design decisions that would be acceptable in other contexts — ambiguous confirmation dialogs, unclear state changes, actions that are hard to undo — are not acceptable when the action being confirmed is a medication dose.

Error prevention in healthcare UX means designing for the wrong action to be hard, not just the right action to be easy. It means confirmation at the right level of friction: high enough that accidental confirmation is unlikely, low enough that the friction itself does not become a workaround driver.

The best clinical interfaces I have worked on share a common characteristic: they were designed by people who spent time watching clinicians work before writing a single requirement. That observation time is expensive. The cost of skipping it is higher.
